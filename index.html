Reference · Test strategy

How to crush the multiple choice

Knowing the facts is half of it. These are the moves that turn facts into points.

01

The five-step attack

  1. Read the source line first. Who wrote it, and when? The date tells you the era, and the era kills half the answers.
  2. Read the question stem before the full passage. Know what you're hunting for.
  3. Predict an answer in your head before you look at the choices.
  4. Eliminate answers from the wrong time period. This is the #1 trick. An answer about the Civil War can't explain something from 1765.
  5. Pick the “big trend” answer. AP answers are usually broad, historically accurate ideas, not weird specific details.
02

Trap answers to watch for

  • True but irrelevant: a correct fact that doesn't answer this question.
  • Wrong era: the right idea, the wrong decade.
  • Extreme words: “always,” “never,” “completely,” “all.” History is messy, and these are usually wrong.
  • Reversed cause/effect: e.g. “Shays' Rebellion was caused by the Constitution.” Nope, it was the other way around.
  • Out of the source's mind: the answer must match what that author would actually believe.
03

HIPP: decode any source

Historical situation: what's going on at the time?
Intended audience: who's it written for?
Purpose: why was it written? To persuade? Complain? Justify?
Point of view: who is the author, and how does that shape it?

Example: a 1765 pamphlet by a Boston merchant about the Stamp Act → situation: post-war British taxes · purpose: rally boycotts · POV: a merchant who loses money.

04

The four thinking moves AP tests

Causation: what caused it, what did it cause? (Think in chains!)

Comparison: how were things similar or different? (Spanish vs. French vs. English colonies.)

Continuity & Change: what stayed the same and what changed? (Slavery after 1865 → sharecropping.)

Contextualization: what bigger picture was this part of? (Seneca Falls ← Second Great Awakening reform wave.)

05

The eight AP themes

Every question fits one of these.

NAT
American & National Identity: who counts as “American”? Citizenship, rights, sectionalism.
WXT
Work, Exchange & Technology: economy, labor, inventions (cotton gin, Erie Canal).
GEO
Geography & Environment: how land, climate and resources shaped lives.
MIG
Migration & Settlement: who moved, why, and what happened.
PCE
Politics & Power: government, parties, laws, the struggle over power.
WOR
America in the World: wars, treaties, foreign policy.
ARC
American & Regional Culture: religion, art, ideas (Great Awakening, Transcendentalism).
SOC
Social Structures: class, race, gender, family.
06

If there's a written part: the thesis formula

“Although [counter-point], [main claim] because [reason 1] and [reason 2].”

Example: “Although the Missouri Compromise temporarily calmed tensions, westward expansion was the main cause of the Civil War because new territories forced the nation to repeatedly fight over slavery, as seen in the Compromise of 1850 and the Kansas-Nebraska Act.”

Always use specific evidence (names, laws, dates) and explain how it proves your point. Evidence without explanation earns zero.

07

Test-day game plan

  • Roughly 1 minute per question. Stuck for more than 90 seconds? Mark it, guess, move on, come back.
  • Never leave a blank. There's no penalty for guessing.
  • Eat breakfast, drink water, sleep the night before. Your brain is a muscle; feed it.
  • If you blank out: breathe in for 4, hold 4, out 4. Then go back to the source date and eliminate by era.
